Rural Community Immigration Class
Class
87.4 (1) For the purposes of subsection 12(2) of the Act, the rural community immigration class is prescribed as a class of persons who may become permanent residents on the basis of their ability to become economically established in Canada.
Member of class
(2) A foreign national is a member of the rural community immigration class if they demonstrate that they meet all of the following requirements from the date on which they apply for permanent resident status until they obtain that status:
(a) they intend to reside within the geographic boundaries of a Canadian community that is designated by the Minister under section 87.41;
(b) they have a valid certificate of recommendation issued by an economic development organization in accordance with section 87.44;
(c) they meet the work experience requirements set out in subsection 87.45(1) or are exempt from them under subsection 87.45(3);
(d) they have an eligible offer of employment that meets the requirements set out in section 87.46;
(e) they hold, as evidence of their education, either
(i) a Canadian educational credential, or
(ii) a foreign diploma, certificate or credential that is at least equivalent to a Canadian secondary school diploma and an equivalency assessment that is less than five years old on the date on which they apply for permanent resident status;
(f) they have had their proficiency in English or French evaluated by an organization or institution that is designated under subsection 74(3) using a language test that is approved under that subsection, the results of which must be less than two years old on the date on which they apply for permanent resident status and must indicate that the foreign national has met the applicable threshold that is fixed by the Minister under subsection 74(1) for each of the four language skill areas;
(g) they have unless they are authorized to work and already working in Canada on the date on which they apply for permanent resident status in the form of transferable and available funds, unencumbered by debts or other obligations, an amount equal to one half of the amount identified, in the most recent edition of the publication concerning low-income cut-offs published annually by Statistics Canada under the Statistics Act , for rural areas outside census metropolitan areas or census agglomerations, as the minimum amount of before-tax annual income that is necessary to support a group of persons equal in number to the total number of the applicant and their family members, whether their family members are accompanying them or not;
(h) if they are in Canada, they have valid temporary resident status.
Designation of community
87.41 (1) The Minister may designate a community outside of Quebec if it is rural, taking into consideration demographic, geographic and other factors.
Factors
(2) The factors the Minister may consider when deciding whether to designate a rural community include
(a) the economic circumstances of the community;
(b) the community’s capacity to welcome and settle new immigrants; and
(c) the community’s administrative capacity to participate in the rural community immigration program.
Public notice
(3) The Minister must make the names of communities that are designated available on the Department’s website.
Economic development organizations
87.42 (1) The Minister may designate economic development organizations for the purpose of making recommendations with respect to foreign nationals who wish to apply for the rural community immigration class with respect to a designated community.
Clarification
(2) For greater certainty, the designation does not make an organization an agent of His Majesty or a Crown corporation , as defined in section 2 of the Financial Administration Act , and does not make its officers and employees members of the federal public administration.
Priority sectors and occupations
(3) An economic development organization ceases to be designated if it does not establish a list of priority sectors and occupations that, in its opinion, will best contribute to economic development in the designated community and review and submit that list to the Minister annually.
Charging fees prohibited
(4) To maintain its designation, an economic development organization must not charge fees for
(a) designating an employer or revoking the designation of an employer under section 87.43; or
(b) issuing, amending or revoking a certificate of recommendation.
Designation of employer
87.43 (1) An economic development organization must designate an employer as eligible to participate in the rural community immigration program if the employer applies to be designated and meets all of the following criteria:
(a) it carries on a genuine business
(i) that has been in continuous active operation under the same ownership for at least two years within the designated community with respect to which the economic development organization is designated, or
(ii) that has been in continuous active operation in any location and that, in the opinion of the economic development organization, either will make a substantial contribution to the designated community’s economy by continuing operations in the community or relocating to it or has already made such a substantial contribution;
(b) it carries on business in relation to at least one sector or occupation that is set out in the priority list established by the economic development organization;
(c) a representative of the employer has successfully completed intercultural competency training;
(d) a representative of the employer has successfully completed onboarding training;
(e) it commits to supporting the settlement of each foreign national to whom it makes an eligible offer of employment and any accompanying family members, including by facilitating access to settlement support;
(f) it is not in contravention of worker protection laws;
(g) it is not
(i) a designated economic development organization,
(ii) a consulate,
(iii) an employer referred to in paragraph 200(3)(g.1) or (h),
(iv) a business that recruits individuals in order to establish a pool of candidates who are intended to be transferred or contracted to other businesses, or
(v) a business owned in whole or in part by a member of a law society of a province or the Chambre des notaires du Québec who provides services related to immigration or by a member of the College , as defined in section 2 of the College of Immigration and Citizenship Consultants Act .
Revocation of designation
(2) An economic development organization may revoke the designation of an employer if
(a) the economic development organization becomes aware that an order has been made against the designated employer or an administrative monetary penalty has been imposed on it because it is in violation of the employer compliance regime established under the Act;
(b) the economic development organization becomes aware of misrepresentation in the employer’s application for designation or application for a certificate of recommendation or in an eligible offer of employment;
(c) the economic development organization becomes aware that a body responsible for the administration of worker protection laws has found the designated employer to be non-compliant with those laws;
(d) the economic development organization becomes aware that the designated employer no longer meets the designation criteria set out in subsection (1);
(e) the designated employer or any person who recruits a foreign national for the designated employer has, directly or indirectly, charged or recovered fees from the foreign national; or
(f) the designated employer has voluntarily requested to withdraw from participating in the rural community immigration program.
Definition of worker protection laws
(3) In this section, worker protection laws means any federal or provincial laws that protect human rights or regulate the employment or recruitment of employees in a province in which the employer carries on business.
Certificate of recommendation
87.44 (1) An economic development organization may issue a certificate of recommendation to a foreign national if a designated employer applies for a certificate with respect to the foreign national and the economic development organization is satisfied that the foreign national
(a) has an eligible offer of employment that meets the requirements set out in section 87.46 and the eligible offer
(i) is for work in a sector or occupation that is set out in the priority list established by the economic development organization,
(ii) sets out a wage that is at least as high as the highest of
(A) the applicable minimum wage,
(B) the lowest wage in the range of wages set out in the Job Bank website of the Canada Employment Insurance Commission for the occupation referred to in the offer, and
(C) any higher wage that the economic development organization has established as an acceptable wage on the basis of the local labour market, and
(iii) is supported by documentation of recruitment efforts and any other supplementary documentation that is necessary to demonstrate that the offer of employment is an eligible offer of employment referred to in section 87.46;
(b) has the required work experience for the TEER Category of the occupation listed in the eligible offer of employment as set out in subsection 87.45(1) or is exempt from the required work experience under subsection 87.45(3);
(c) holds, as evidence of their education, either
(i) a Canadian educational credential, or
(ii) a foreign diploma, certificate or credential that is at least equivalent to a Canadian secondary school diploma and an equivalency assessment that is less than five years old on the date on which the economic development organization expects they will apply for permanent resident status;
(d) have had their proficiency in English or French evaluated by an organization or institution that is designated under subsection 74(3) using a language test that is approved under that subsection, the results of which must be less than two years old on the date on which the economic development organization expects they will apply for permanent resident status and must indicate that the foreign national has met the applicable threshold that is fixed by the Minister under subsection 74(1) for each of the four language skill areas; and
(e) intends to reside in the community with respect to which the economic development organization is designated.
Prioritizing applications
(2) When issuing a certificate of recommendation, an economic development organization may prioritize applications in order to maximize economic development in a community with respect to which it is designated and to meet the needs of the local labour market. This prioritization may be based on a foreign national’s occupation, wage, work experience, language proficiency in English or French, education and connection to that community.
Revocation criteria not met
(3) The economic development organization must revoke a certificate of recommendation if it becomes aware that the foreign national did not meet the criteria set out in subsection (1) at the time the certificate was issued or no longer meets those criteria at any time before they obtain permanent resident status.
Amendment
(4) If a foreign national obtains a new eligible offer of employment in accordance with subsection 87.46(3), the economic development organization may amend a certificate of recommendation to be based on the new eligible offer of employment.
Revocation employer no longer designated
(5) The economic development organization must revoke a certificate of recommendation if it is based on an eligible offer of employment from an employer whose designation has been revoked.
Validity period
(6) A certificate of recommendation is valid until the earlier of
(a) the first anniversary of the day on which it is issued, and
(b) the day on which the economic development organization revokes it.
Work experience
87.45 (1) A foreign national meets the work experience requirement if they have work experience that satisfies the criteria set out in subsection (2) in one of the following occupations:
(a) for an eligible offer of employment in TEER Category 0 or 1, an occupation that is listed in TEER Category 0, 1, 2 or 3;
(b) for an eligible offer of employment in TEER Category 2, an occupation that is listed in TEER Category 1, 2, 3 or 4;
(c) for an eligible offer of employment in unit group 33102 or 44101 of the National Occupational Classification , an occupation that corresponds to unit group 31301 of the National Occupational Classification or that is listed in TEER Category 2, 3 or 4;
(d) for any other eligible offer of employment in TEER Category 3 or 4, an occupation that is listed in TEER Category 2, 3 or 4;
(e) for an eligible offer of employment in TEER Category 5, an occupation that is in the same National Occupational Classification unit group as the offer.
Work experience criteria
(2) The work experience must
(a) total at least one year of full-time work, or the equivalent in part-time work, in an occupation listed in the National Occupational Classification ;
(b) have been acquired in the three years before the day on which the foreign national applies for permanent resident status or, if the foreign national has not yet applied for that status, the day on which the economic development organization expects they will apply;
(c) include the performance of the actions described in the lead statement for the occupation as set out in the National Occupational Classification ;
(d) include the performance of a substantial number of the main duties of the occupation as set out in the National Occupational Classification ;
(e) include only work that was done in the employ of a third party, unless it was acquired as a medical practitioner entitled to practise medicine under the laws of the jurisdiction in which it was acquired; and
(f) if it was acquired in Canada, have been acquired while the foreign national was authorized to work in Canada and had temporary resident status.
Exception eligible credential
(3) The work experience requirement does not apply to a foreign national who obtained an eligible credential from a post-secondary program and who meets all of the following requirements:
(a) they were authorized to study in Canada and had temporary resident status during the entire period of training or study;
(b) they were enrolled as a full-time student during the entire period of the program;
(c) they obtained the credential during the 18-month period that preceded the day on which they applied for permanent resident status or, if the foreign national has not yet applied for that status, the day on which the economic development organization expects they will apply;
(d) in the case of a foreign national who completed a master’s or doctoral degree program of less than two years in duration, the foreign national was physically present in the designated community where the offered employment is located during the entire period of the program;
(e) in the case of a foreign national who completed a post-secondary program other than one referred to in paragraph (d), the program was of at least two years in duration and, during the 24-month period that immediately preceded the day on which the eligible credential was obtained, the foreign national was physically present for at least 16 months in the designated community where the offered employment is located.
Definition of eligible credential
(4) In subsection (3), eligible credential means a degree, diploma, certificate, trade credential or apprenticeship credential from a Canadian public post-secondary institution in the designated community where the offered employment is located, but does not include a credential obtained in a study or training program
(a) in which the study of English or French as a second language amounted to more than half of the credits required to complete the program;
(b) in which distance learning amounted to more than half of the credits required to complete the program; or
(c) for which the foreign national received a scholarship or fellowship that stipulated that they must return to their home country to apply the knowledge and skills gained there.
Eligible offer of employment
87.46 (1) An offer of employment is eligible if
(a) it is made by an employer that
(i) is a designated employer under subsection 87.43(1) at the time the offer is made,
(ii) is actively engaged in the business in respect of which the offer is made, and
(iii) is not a business in which a majority of voting or ownership interests are held, either individually or collectively, by the foreign national or their spouse or common-law partner or that is controlled, directly or indirectly, by the foreign national or their spouse or common-law partner;
(b) it is consistent with the reasonable employment needs of the designated employer;
(c) it contains terms that the designated employer is reasonably able to fulfill;
(d) it promises a wage above or within the range of wages set out in the Job Bank website of the Canada Employment Insurance Commission for the occupation referred to in the offer;
(e) it does not require proficiency in a language other than English or French as a condition of employment; and
(f) it offers employment
(i) that is non-seasonal, full-time and for a continuous and indeterminate period,
(ii) that requires at least 75% of work hours to be spent in the designated community, and
(iii) that the foreign national has the qualifications and ability to perform and is likely to accept and carry out, as indicated by their past work experience or education related to the lead statement and main duties of the occupation as set out in the National Occupational Classification .
Ministerial decision
(2) The Minister may conclude that an offer of employment is not eligible if the designated employer is found to have contravened worker protection laws , as defined in subsection 87.43(3).
New offer
(3) The eligible offer of employment that was first submitted with a foreign national’s application for permanent resident status must be used to evaluate the application, unless the foreign national receives a new eligible offer of employment from a different designated employer, in the following circumstances:
(a) after the application is made, the employer who made the existing offer of employment becomes an employer referred to in subparagraph 200(3)(h)(ii) or (iii);
(b) the foreign national is working for the employer under a work permit and, after the application is made, a new work permit is issued to the applicant in the circumstances described in subsection 207.1(1); or
(c) an economic development organization revokes the employer’s designation and the foreign national did not contribute to the actions of the employer that led to the revocation.

REGULATORY IMPACT ANALYSIS STATEMENT
( This statement is not part of the Regulations. )
Executive summary
Issues: Rural communities across Canada are experiencing economic and demographic challenges, including aging populations, low birth rates, limited immigration, and higher out-migration. Since 1998, efforts have been made through the Provincial Nominee Program (PNP) to spread the benefits of immigration across the country. To date, these efforts have resulted in an increase in newcomers settling outside of Ontario, Quebec, and British Columbia, from 11% in 1998 to 30% in 2025. While these regional immigration programs have made progress in better distributing newcomers across Canada, most newcomers still tend to concentrate in major urban areas. This results in an uneven distribution of economic immigrants across Canada, leading to persistent regional labour market shortages, employers having difficulties filling key jobs, and ongoing challenges for local economic development.
Description: The Regulations Amending the Immigration and Refugee Protection Regulations (the Regulations) will support the creation of a permanent economic immigration program by establishing a new rural community immigration economic class. The Regulations set the eligibility requirements for permanent residency through the Rural Community Immigration (RCI) Program, as well as the responsibilities and requirements for participating communities in the program. The Minister may also designate new communities based on factors set out in the Regulations.
Rationale: The Amendments are expected to result in costs to the Government of Canada, provincial governments, Economic Development Organizations (EDOs), and employers who utilize the RCI Program. These costs are estimated at $60.8 million in present value (PV) over 10 periods of 12 months. The benefits of the Amendments consist of ongoing cost savings for Canadian provinces, employers, and RCI Program applicants, and total $50.3 million PV over 10 periods of 12 months. Therefore, the Amendments will result in a net cost of $10.5 million PV. However, there are also many qualitative benefits to employers, RCI applicants, and EDOs, such as; ensuring that immigration is aligned with local economic development needs, increased employee satisfaction, and strengthened workforce stability, which are expected to result in an overall net benefit for Canadians.
Issues
Canada’s ability to support balanced regional growth is being challenged by persistent disparities in where newcomers settle. In 2021, more than 78% of newcomers settled in Canada’s 10 largest cities, which currently hold approximately 56% of the Canadian population . Recent adjustments to Canada’s immigration system, including reductions to permanent resident admissions growth and new limits on temporary resident arrivals beginning in 2024, have affected labour supply differently across regions.
Labour market data consistently shows that many rural and remote regions experience structurally tight labour markets, characterized by lower unemployment rates ( under 5% as of June 2026 ), limited local labour mobility, and a smaller working-age population than urban areas . These conditions are compounded by an aging population; people aged 65 and older make up 23.2% of the rural population, compared to 18.2% in urban centres. Rural areas are also experiencing a departure of skilled workers from key sectors such as health care , trades, agriculture, natural resources, and manufacturing , as many young workers seek out additional opportunities in larger urban centres.
Background
The Rural and Northern Immigration Pilot (RNIP) was launched in 2019 through Ministerial Instructions issued under section 14.1 of the Immigration and Refugee Protection Act . This section allows the Minister to issue instructions to establish classes of permanent residents as part of the economic class for a limited duration of five years. The RNIP was established with the objective of testing a community-led model for economic immigrant attraction and retention in rural and northern communities by partnering directly with communities represented by local economic development organizations (EDOs). The primary goals of this pilot were to support local economic development and facilitate long-term retention within participating communities. Program results indicated that the RNIP pilot was successful, as demonstrated by the following results:
Over 12 500 skilled workers and their families were admitted through the pilot since 2019.
Over 2 800 businesses were able to utilize the pilot to fill critical local labour market needs.
Over 20% of job offers were in the health care and social services sector.
A preliminary survey showed that 87% of respondents had stayed and planned to continue to live in their RNIP community.
The success of the RNIP has generated significant stakeholder support for continued immigration programming in rural communities. Participating communities have consistently indicated that the pilot has helped them address persistent labour shortages, supported local economic development, and improved newcomer retention outcomes through its community-driven design. Community partners and employers have emphasized that the RNIP model enables better alignment between immigration selection and local labour market needs, while also strengthening settlement and integration supports at the local level.
In December 2024, Immigration, Refugees, and Citizenship Canada (IRCC or the Department) launched the Rural Community Immigration Pilot (RCI Pilot) as an interim measure to bridge the service gap following the conclusion of the Rural and Northern Immigration Pilot (RNIP), while work continued to design a permanent community-based immigration program (as noted in the public announcement of the pilot in January 2025). The RCI Pilot ensured continued access to a dedicated regional economic immigration pathway for rural communities, including small and remote communities facing persistent labour shortages, while avoiding disruption for employers and communities that would have been unable to fill key labour market gaps without the pilot.
The launch of the RCI Pilot also provided IRCC with an opportunity to apply lessons learned from the implementation of the RNIP, as well as from other successful regional economic initiatives, such as the Atlantic Immigration Program (AIP). Drawing on program evaluations, operational experience, and stakeholder feedback, the Department introduced a series of targeted program refinements aimed at strengthening integrity, improving consistency across communities, and enhancing overall operational efficiency, while preserving the RCI Pilot’s core objective of supporting newcomer attraction and retention in small, rural, and remote areas.
Currently, 14 rural and remote communities footnote 2 are participating in the RCI Pilot, with early evidence indicating strong first-year results across key indicators, including employer participation, alignment with local labour market needs, and community capacity to support newcomer settlement. These results reinforce earlier findings from the RNIP that community-driven, place-based immigration approaches can effectively support economic growth and retention in rural and remote regions.
Objective
The primary objective of this program is to support sustained economic development in rural communities, including small and remote communities, by providing a permanent immigration pathway that responds directly to local and regional labour market needs. By enabling communities to identify priority sectors and occupations and attract skilled workers aligned with those needs, the Rural Community Immigration Program (RCI Program) is intended to help employers address persistent labour shortages, support business growth, and strengthen the economic resilience of smaller communities over time.
A second core objective is to support long-term demographic sustainability in participating communities through the successful settlement and retention of newcomers and their families. Many rural and remote regions face population decline and accelerated aging, which undermines the availability of workers and the long-term viability of local services and infrastructure. By combining employment opportunities with coordinated settlement and integration supports, the program aims to encourage permanent settlement, strengthen community attachment, and support population growth in areas that have historically struggled to attract and retain newcomers.
This program seeks to more effectively distribute the benefits of economic immigration across Canada beyond major urban centres. By embedding immigration selection at the community level and aligning admissions with local economic priorities, the RCI Program will help ensure that the economic and social benefits of immigration such as labour force growth, business development, and community vitality are shared more equitably across regions. This objective supports broader federal goals related to sustainable rural economic development and inclusive growth, as well as the long-term viability of Canada’s immigration system as a national economic tool.
Description
The Regulations establish the Rural Community Immigration Class, which provides a pathway to permanent residence for skilled foreign workers (and their spouses and/or dependents) who can help address identified local labour market needs and who intend to reside in participating communities. Under the Regulations, the Minister may designate EDOs outside of Quebec, based on geographic and demographic factors, and other factors, including economic and administrative capacity considerations. EDOs will be responsible for making recommendations with respect to foreign nationals who wish to apply for the RCI Program with respect to a designated community and annually establishing a list of priority sectors and occupations that align with local labour market gaps and economic development objectives in their community.
Applicants seeking permanent residence through the program will be required to
Have a valid certificate of recommendation issued by a designated economic development organization;
Have an admissible job offer that meets the wage requirements in one of the identified priority sectors or occupations;
Demonstrate that they meet the requirements relating to
Work experience;
Education;
Language proficiency in one of Canada’s official languages; and
Proof of settlement funds;
Intend to live and work in the recommending community; and
If the candidate is already in Canada, hold valid temporary resident status. Candidates can apply both from within Canada and from outside of Canada.
EDOs are required to conduct formal employer designation processes to determine which employers are eligible to participate in the program and may refuse to designate or revoke designation from employers found to be ineligible. To be eligible to participate in the RCI Program, designated employers will be required to complete onboarding immigration training, ensuring a clear understanding of program requirements and responsibilities, as well as intercultural competency training to promote inclusive workplaces and strengthen employers’ ability to support newcomers. Designated employers will be required to support the settlement of each foreign national and any accompanying family members, including by facilitating access to settlement and social service support.
Regulatory development
Consultation
Community partners, provinces with participating communities, and regional development agencies representing economic development interests across Canada were consulted on the regulatory amendments in Summer 2026. Provinces and territories without participating communities were not directly consulted. Feedback from community partners was overwhelmingly positive, with communities highlighting that the pilot program’s design has been effective in meeting its stated objectives. In particular, the priority setting exercise was highlighted by multiple communities as an effective tool in meeting local labour market needs. The program design highlighted by communities will be maintained in the Regulations to ensure a smooth transition between the RCI Pilot and RCI Program. Some communities highlighted concerns over access to ongoing funding for positions within their EDO responsible for administering the pilot, which is outside of the scope of the creation of a new rural economic class in the Regulations. Although this is outside of the scope of the Regulations, communities may engage with regional development agencies to support their implementation of the program.
Provinces with participating communities were also engaged on the regulatory amendments. Some provinces highlighted the complementarity of the program to their own Provincial Nominee Program (PNP) streams particularly in provinces where there is no dedicated rural stream. In provinces where there was a rural stream, stakeholders highlighted that any new designated communities should be done in concert with provinces to ensure that there is no duplication of pathways. While this is outside of the scope of the regulatory amendments, this feedback will continue to inform collaboration between levels of government on rural immigration going forward. Some provinces also highlighted that managing the priorities of many different communities within their jurisdiction through existing channels posed an administrative burden, so the regulatory amendments were welcomed as an opportunity to download the identification of local labour market priorities directly to communities, who have a much better understanding of local labour market needs.
Provinces highlighted the risk of misuse of the program with the possibility of fraudulent employers attempting to access the program, as this is seen in their own PNP/rural PNP streams. This will be addressed through the regulatory amendments as part of the employer designation revocation process that is built into the program requirements, which has been well-managed by communities and is effective at addressing misrepresentation. The employer designation process allows communities to verify the legitimacy of an employer before they are able to access the program, limiting the ability of illegitimate or misrepresentative employers to access the program and apply for recommendations. Provinces also highlighted their concern that there was a limited role for the provinces in setting the direction of this program. While this feedback has been taken into consideration, the goal of the Regulations is to allow communities to have a lead role in setting their economic development goals, which may differ from broader provincial goals. The Regulations reflect the key partnership between communities and IRCC, and provinces have a limited, informal role to play in the actual program delivery. Provinces and territories that do not have participating communities were not consulted on the regulatory amendments, however will be consulted ahead of any new community designations within their jurisdiction.
Some provinces also emphasized that the PNP should remain the primary regional economic immigration pathway, with the proposed RCI Program serving as a complementary program rather than a substitute. IRCC reaffirmed that this aligns with the proposal’s intended design. The RCI Program is deliberately modest in scale and focused on supporting the unique labour market needs of rural communities. A key feature of the RCI Program is its reliance on local partnerships and community-led efforts to support the settlement and integration of newcomers. IRCC noted that significantly expanding the program could risk diluting this community-driven approach, which is central to its success.
Regional development agencies, including Atlantic Canada Opportunities Agency (ACOA), Canadian Northern Economic Development Agency (CanNor), Federal Economic Development Agency for Southern Ontario (FedDev Ontario), Federal Economic Development Agency for Northern Ontario (FedNor), Prairies Economic Development Canada (PrairiesCan), and Pacific Economic Development Canada (PacifiCan), were also engaged on the regulatory amendments. The feedback received across all regional development agencies was extremely positive, with all agencies supportive of the amendment. Northern stakeholders highlighted the RCI Program as a valuable opportunity to strengthen workforce development efforts and help address persistent labour shortages across Northern and territorial communities. Stakeholders noted that increased access to skilled workers could support local employers, enhance economic resilience, and contribute to the long-term sustainability of Northern communities, should there be an opportunity for those communities to participate in the future. Several agencies also pointed to upcoming major federal projects in their regions that may benefit from enhanced access to skilled talent in rural and remote areas. Agencies also emphasized their support for ongoing engagement and collaboration with provincial and territorial counterparts to ensure alignment and complementarity between immigration pathways.
The consultations reflected stakeholder support of the regulatory amendments and the ongoing commitment by all partners to a collaborative approach to rural immigration. Many of the successful features of the existing pilot that stakeholders highlighted are being maintained through the Regulations in the new program. Concerns that fall outside of the scope of the regulatory amendments, including funding arrangements for local delivery partners and broader program administration and implementation matters, will continue to be addressed through ongoing collaboration with participating communities.
Indigenous engagement, consultation and modern treaty obligations
As required by the Cabinet Directive on the Federal Approach to Modern Treaty Implementation , an assessment of modern treaty implications was conducted on the proposal. The assessment examined the geographical scope and subject matter of the Regulations in relation to modern treaties in effect and did not identify any modern treaty implications or obligations. The RCI Program will not operate within any jurisdictions with a modern treaty or self-government agreement and no expansion that would overlap with modern treaty/self-governing areas is planned.
Instrument choice
The authority to create a new class of permanent residents is prescribed by the Immigration and Refugee Protection Act . Amending the Immigration and Refugee Protection Regulations is the most appropriate way to create a new permanent economic immigration class. The authority to create an immigration class through Ministerial Instructions is limited, both in time and scope, while an amendment to the Immigration and Refugee Protection Regulations allows the created class to be established permanently.
Regulatory analysis
Benefits and costs
The Regulations would have significant cost implications for IRCC, provincial governments, Economic Development Organizations (EDOs), and employers who utilize the RCI Program, estimated at $60.8 million Present Value (PV) over 10 periods of 12 months. Total monetized benefits are estimated at $50.3 million PV, resulting in a net cost of $10.5 million PV over 10 years. However, there are many qualitative benefits of the proposal, which are expected to result in an overall positive net benefit for Canadians.
An important first step in developing a cost-benefit methodology is establishing a baseline scenario against which options may be measured. For this analysis, the baseline scenario is one where the RCI Program does not exist; therefore, foreign nationals must apply to work in Canada through different immigration programs. Following engagement with subject matter experts, the Provincial Nominee Program (PNP) was used as the baseline scenario, as it was identified as the most likely alternative program to be utilized by proposed RCI Program candidates.
While continued pilots are a potential option for the baseline, they are not suitable in the long term. Experience shows that pilots are costly to develop and maintain. They are also, by nature, intended to generate evidence, not serve as long term solutions, as they run for a limited amount of time (five years in this case) and then sunset or become a permanent program. Similar to other regulatory amendments IRCC has done previously to make pilots permanent programs (most recently the AIP program in 2022, the baseline is chosen as the most likely alternative existing avenue that candidates would come through, in the absence of the pilot program. Continued pilots are not a reasonable assumption, since by design they are temporary and experimental, intended to test approaches before a decision is made on whether they should be made permanent programs. Continued pilots would likely make employers, provinces and EDOs reluctant to repeatedly invest in pathways they expect will end in a short number of years. Additionally, economic pilots established under the MIs are limited to 2 750 admissions per year, which severely limits any kind of growth under the pilot. Continued pilots can also create instability in immigration planning, create repeated uncertainty for candidates, and the continuously changing eligibility criteria, application processes, reporting requirements, etc., that would all be part of continuous different pilot programs would not be advisable. Furthermore, continued use of pilots to target specific groups could be seen as IRCC seeking to circumvent the reg process and "cheating" the 5-year expiry rule. Pilots can only run for five years, after which they sunset. For a pilot to continue, it must either be transitioned into a permanent program, or change substantially enough to be launched as a new five-year pilot.
Both the RCI Program and PNP are similar in terms of their objectives to fill local labour market needs and distribute the benefits of immigration across Canada by empowering either provincial/territorial, or Economic Development Organizations (EDOs) and employer partners. However, there are some key differences in Program design. First, under the PNP, provinces and territories are required to assess the language, education, and work experience credentials of applicants. Under the RCI Program, these criteria will be initially evaluated by participating EDOs and formally verified against admissibility criteria by the federal government. Second, employers will play a more active role in the RCI Program, and will be required to take mandatory onboarding training, intercultural competency training, and support the settlement and integration of foreign nationals hired under the program. These elements are not required for employers of workers in the PNP.
The baseline scenario is then compared with the regulatory scenario, in which IRCC implements a permanent RCI Program.
Assumptions for the analysis were based on publicly available information, Immigration, Refugees and Citizenship Canada (IRCC) datasets, and subject matter expertise. The results are discussed below. For further details regarding methodology, the full cost-benefit analysis is available upon request from IRCC, at IRCC.RCIProgram-ProgrammeICR.IRCC@cic.gc.ca .
The cost-benefit analysis makes assumptions around variables that may be subject to uncertainty. It is important to acknowledge this uncertainty, in particular the presence of uncertainty in variables due to a lack of historical data. For this reason, a sensitivity analysis was conducted to examine how changes in uncertain variables would impact the cost-benefit analysis results. Sensitivity analysis results are discussed in the relevant section below.
Note that, due to rounding, there are some instances throughout the cost-benefit analysis where components do not exactly sum to totals, in particular when comparing costs in the small business lens to the overarching costs in the main costs and benefits section.
Costs
The Regulations will result in costs for IRCC, provincial governments, employers, and EDOs. No costs to applicants are expected, since there is no fee to apply for recommendation under the RCI program, and the estimated time to complete an application is the same for both the PNP and RCI program.
Costs to IRCC
Incremental costs to IRCC would consist of both transition and ongoing costs. Transition costs would include costs for developing communications products, updates to program delivery instructions and other administrative materials, and costs to issue program guidance to EDOs. These costs will be incurred in the year 2026 and are estimated at approximately $0.24 million.
Ongoing costs to IRCC reflect the additional cost burden of admitting individuals to Canada via the proposed program, rather than through the PNP. Total ongoing costs to IRCC are estimated as $7.0 million PV over 10 years. While these costs primarily stem from costs to coordinate and support communities participating in the program, there are also ongoing costs to conduct data analysis and support employers, including support via the Dedicated Service Channel (DSC).
To estimate costs to IRCC, the expected number of recommendations issued annually between the RCI Pilot and Francophone Community Immigration Pilot was first calculated. This estimate is based on a target of 3000 admissions per year, including Principal Applicants and dependents. A further breakdown of target admissions by pilot program is not available. This assumption is based on IRCC’s Departmental Multi-Year Levels Pan for 2026 2028, the most current approved Plan at the time of this analysis.
Costs to provincial governments
The implementation of a permanent RCI program will present ongoing costs for provincial governments in the form of foregone revenue, estimated as $23.4 million PV over 10 years. Whilst PNP applicants are required to pay a fee to provincial governments when applying for nomination, footnote 3 RCI applicants will not be subject to these fees. The reason for this distinction is that while the PNP is governed by the provinces and territories themselves, the RCI program will be a federal program co-administered with community partners and subject to a different legislative framework.
Costs to employers
The Regulations will also present ongoing costs for employers who choose to utilize the RCI Program, estimated at $24.0 million PV over 10 years. These costs reflect additional requirements for employers to recruit candidates and issue job offers, apply to communities for designation, complete mandatory onboarding and intercultural competency training, evaluate applicants against eligibility requirements, and to connect candidates and their families with settlement services to support their retention and integration.
As the pilot has only been implemented since January 2025, data on the volume of active employers, newly designated employers, and recommendation volumes are limited. Based on the available data, these costs were computed using the assumption of 260 new designated employers each year, and 2 760 recommendations issued each year for all employers actively using the RCI Program.
Since there is uncertainty surrounding the number of employers that will be impacted due to a lack of historical data and how much time they can expect to spend on recruitment, a sensitivity analysis on relevant variables can be found below.
Furthermore, as detailed in the Distributional Analysis in the following section, while the RCI Program is expected to benefit Canada overall by spreading the benefits of immigration to areas where it is most needed via a targeted approach, it is acknowledged that the RCI Program will result in a decrease in immigration volumes to most provinces and territories. While it is possible that this decrease may negatively impact these local economies through a loss in employment, this is not likely, due to the relatively small change in volumes expected relative to the total population of each affected province/territory.
Costs to Economic Development Organizations
The regulatory proposal will also present costs for Economic Development Organizations within each community, estimated at $6.1 million PV annually. These include costs to establish priority industries and determine how best to utilize their community’s allocation to achieve their economic goals. There are also costs for the assessment and evaluation of designated employers, candidate recommendations, and job offers.
Benefits
The anticipated benefits of a permanent RCI Program are informed by the demonstrated outcomes of its predecessor initiatives, including the RNIP and RCI Pilot. These initiatives have provided strong evidence of the program design’s effectiveness in supporting community driven immigration, addressing local labour market needs, and fostering successful newcomer attraction and retention. Building on this experience, similar benefits would be expected to be realized through the establishment of the RCI Pilot as a permanent program.
Expected benefits include ongoing cost savings for Canadian provinces, employers, and RCI Program applicants, and total $50.3 million PV over 10 periods of 12 months. There would also be additional qualitative benefits to employers, candidates, and participating rural communities, which are also discussed below.
Benefits to provincial governments
The Regulations will benefit provincial governments through a reduction in processing costs, estimated at $4.8 million PV over 10 periods of 12 months. Benefits are calculated assuming that the expected volume of applicants who would be assumed to enter Canada each year under the RCI Program, would come through the PNP if the proposed regulatory amendments were not implemented.
While provinces are responsible for processing PNP applications, EDOs will be responsible for processing RCI Program recommendation applications. Specifically, under the RCI Program, provinces will not be required to process nomination applications from employers, assess the legitimacy of job offers against program requirements, or provide candidates with Certificates of Nomination.
Benefits to employers
The Regulations are expected to deliver significant benefits to employers in participating communities through cost savings for PNP-specific activities, which will not be required of employers under the RCI Program. While mandatory for the PNP, under the RCI Program, employers will not be required to contact Provinces to express an interest in the program. They will also not be required to reach out to Provinces for nominations and job offers for candidates. Cost-savings to employers as a result of not completing these activities are estimated as $23.0 million PV over 10 periods.
Further qualitative benefits to employers would be realized, as the RCI Program will provide a reliable, community-driven pathway to address persistent labour shortages. Building on outcomes from the RNIP and the RCI Pilot, the RCI Program will support rural employers in attracting and retaining skilled workers aligned with local labour market needs. While the PNP also provides employers with immigration benefits to address labour market gaps, the RCI Program will concentrate employer benefits within locally defined priority sectors and occupations, thereby ensuring immigration is closely aligned with local economic development priorities and that employers can access talent where shortages are most acute. Therefore, compared to broader provincial immigration pathways like the PNP, the RCI Program will offer a more targeted and responsive mechanism tailored to rural labour market realities.
Moreover, RCI Program employers will be required to foster inclusive workplaces and facilitate access to settlement services, while collaborating with EDOs and Settlement Provider Organizations to support newcomer integration and long-term retention. These program elements are not required under the PNP, and will benefit employers by increasing employee satisfaction, reducing turnover, and strengthening workforce stability.
In addition, unlike PNP employers, RCI Program employers will benefit from dedicated federal support through the Dedicated Service Channel (DSC). This includes guidance on program requirements and pathway selection support that is especially valuable for small and medium-sized employers with limited capacity or experience in immigration. This support will help employers navigate the immigration process more effectively and efficiently.
Compared to the PNP, employers participating in the RCI Program will have a greater role in the candidate experience. Under the PNP, employers have a more limited and often less formalized role, with less structured support in recruiting, welcoming, and integrating newcomers. Access to provincial streams can also be inconsistent, as they open and close based on shifting priorities, and employers are expected to navigate the system more independently. In contrast, the RCI Program will provide consistent access for rural employers and formalize their involvement by way of designation. This will make hiring foreign workers more accessible for employers, particularly for smaller businesses who may lack the time and resources to manage complex processes on their own.
Overall, it is expected that, though the RCI Program will be more costly for businesses to utilize than the PNP, they will still choose the RCI Program due to the anticipated benefits of improved employee retention, the fact that they will be better equipped to address their labour needs, and the support they will receive from the Dedicated Service Channel. By combining employer leadership, community coordination, and federal support, the RCI Program will enhance employers’ ability to fill critical positions, improve workforce continuity, and support long-term business growth.
Benefits to RCI Program applicants
Implementing the RCI Program as a permanent program will provide many benefits to program candidates. First, candidates will benefit by not having to pay an application (nomination) fee to provincial governments when applying to the RCI Program, while they would if applying to the PNP. Since the RCI Program is a federal program, there is no legislative authority under which candidates can be charged application fees. The total savings benefit to candidates is estimated at $22.5 million PV over 10 years. Note that only the savings to in-Canada applicants are considered in-scope for the purposes of this cost-benefit analysis.
There will also be many qualitative benefits to applicants. The RCI Program will provide a higher level of candidate support than the PNP via stronger employer involvement. Under the RCI Program, employers will be required to take at least one concrete action to foster a welcoming workplace and/or support candidates and their accompanying family members as they settle into the community. Eligible actions include helping candidates secure housing, assisting candidates with transportation (information on public transit, picking up the candidate from the port-of-entry, helping the candidate travel to work or to a local Service Provider Organization (SPO) or essential service), referring candidates to key services in the community (e.g. health card and school registration), and connecting candidates with local groups of interest. This level of engagement is not required under the PNP, where candidates are expected to take greater initiative in their own settlement and integration.
Furthermore, the RCI Program will require that all participating employers be formally designated and vetted by their community. This is not required under the PNP. Designated employers will need to demonstrate an established local presence, provide proof of compliance with employment and occupational health and safety standards, and operate within priority sectors identified through community-led economic planning. They will also be required to complete onboarding and intercultural competency training and commit to supporting newcomer settlement. As a result, candidates will benefit from greater confidence in the stability of their employment, its alignment with local labour market needs, and the employer’s readiness to support a diverse workforce.
Benefits to participating rural communities
The regulatory proposal will also offer significant benefits to participating communities by directly integrating immigration into community-led economic development. The RCI Program will place local labour market needs at the centre of program design, with Economic Development Organizations leading implementation in partnership with IRCC.
Canada as a whole experiences demographic pressures related to an ageing population, low fertility rates, and widespread labour shortages. These challenges are generally more pronounced in rural regions, where long term outmigration, declining population growth, and limited access to talent constrain economic development. The RCI Pilot was designed as a targeted response to these pressures by supporting the attraction and retention of newcomers in communities that have historically benefited less from national immigration flows.
Unlike the PNP, which operates at the provincial/territorial level, the RCI Program will provide Economic Development Organizations with a direct role in shaping immigration outcomes. Through annual priority-setting exercises, Economic Development Organizations will identify in-demand sectors and occupations based on local labour market data, employer engagement, and community consultations. This will ensure that immigration selection is aligned with community-specific economic needs. Under the PNP, priorities are set at broader provincial and federal levels, limiting communities’ influence over candidate selection and reducing alignment with localized challenges.
The RCI Program will also offer benefits to participating communities by increasing the long-term retention of foreign workers in rural communities. The program integrates settlement supports within communities and requires coordination among local partners to support newcomer integration, thereby increasing the likelihood that newcomers will remain in smaller or rural communities. In comparison, the PNP does not include community-driven settlement requirements, and newcomers often settle in larger urban centres, limiting retention in rural areas. Retention issues in rural areas contribute to labour shortages, shrinking social services and an aging population. By increasing retention in these areas, the RCI Program will contribute to a more balanced distribution of economic immigration across Canada while strengthening labour market participation and economic growth in regions facing persistent population challenges.
Canadians
The Regulations will support IRCC’s mandate by strengthening Canada’s managed migration system and advancing regional economic development. At the same time, the RCI Program will reinforce Canada’s commitment to an inclusive and cohesive society. Its focus on welcoming communities, intercultural competency, and coordinated settlement support will promote social inclusion and public confidence in the immigration system, while maintaining Canada’s reputation as a destination of choice for newcomers.

Sensitivity analysis
This cost-benefit analysis makes assumptions around variables that may be subject to uncertainty. It is important to acknowledge this uncertainty, in particular due to the lack of historical data due to the RCI Pilot only just coming into force in December 2025. For this reason, a sensitivity analysis was conducted to examine how changes in uncertain variables could impact the cost-benefit analysis results.
The variables considered for this analysis are the annual increase in designated employers, and the estimated recruitment costs per candidate. As discussed in the cost section above, the central analysis assumes 260 new designated employers each year. Given the absence of historical or proxy time series of a sufficiently reliable length, this assumption may overestimate the impact on employers, as fewer employers may choose to seek designation. Similarly, while participating communities were consulted on employer costs and related program activities, employers themselves were not involved in these consultations. Therefore, while the central analysis assumes 40 hours of recruitment activities per candidate, this may again overestimate the employer’s impacts. Small business lens
The small business lens applies, as there are impacts on small businesses associated with the proposal.
Specifically, there will be costs for all new employers involved in the proposed RCI Program to apply for designation, complete onboarding training required for designation under the proposed program, and to complete intercultural competency training. Businesses will also incur ongoing costs to recruit candidates and issue job offers, confirm applicants meet eligibility requirements and labour market priorities, and to connect candidates and their families with settlement service providers to support their retention and integration.
There will also be significant benefits to all employers in participating communities, including small businesses, through cost savings for PNP-specific activities which will not be required of employers under the RCI Program.
Costs
As the pilot has only been implemented since 2025, data on the volume of active employers, newly designated employers, and recommendation volumes are limited. Based on the available data, the analysis uses the assumption of an initial stock of 1 896 designated employers, and that 63% of designated employers use at least one recommendation each year. In addition, it is assumed that there are 260 new designated employers each year.
While no data exists on the proportion of employers currently participating in the pilot that are small businesses, nor is any utilization frequency available, data from the Temporary Foreign Worker Program suggests that small businesses hire temporary foreign workers at a ratio of 4:10 (i.e. small businesses hire 28.6% of temporary foreign workers). This is used as a proxy for the purposes of the small business lens.
Since for new entrants it is assumed that they are seeking designation for the purposes of hiring foreign workers, this implies that there will be 74 new small business employers each year seeking designation which will incur costs following implementation of the regulatory proposal. Furthermore, it is therefore assumed that there are 542 small businesses in the initial stock of designated employers, and that 789 recommendations would be issued by small businesses each year.
In order to estimate the number of unique small businesses that would incur costs in a given year, IRCC must consider both new small business entrants applying for designation, as well as current designated small business employers. There is no data available on business turnover (businesses which would no longer actively participate in the program) and the decision to hire foreign workers is not a random event (therefore, assuming that participating employers in a given year were randomly distributed would not be a reasonable assumption). Indeed, it is more likely that the same subset of employers choose to repeatedly participate in the program and, therefore, it is assumed that 63% of the 542 small businesses who use a recommendation the first year would be impacted, i.e. 344 employers. Combining this with the 74 new small business entrants each year implies that a total of 1 087 unique small business employers would be impacted by the Regulations over 10 years (small difference due to rounding). Note that IRCC does not need to consider the employers in periods 2 10 who would utilize a recommendation in this calculation, since all employers beyond the initial stock are already counted as impacted in the calculation of new entrants seeking designation, and it is assumed that the same employers each year will be actively seeking to hire foreign workers.
Benefits
As discussed previously, the Regulations are also expected to deliver significant benefits to all employers in participating communities, including small businesses, through cost savings for PNP-specific activities which will not be required of employers under the RCI Program. While mandatory for the PNP, under the RCI Program, employers will not be required to contact provinces to express an interest in the program. They will also not be required to reach out to provinces for nominations and job offers for candidates, or assist candidates with the application process. Cost savings to small businesses as a result of not completing these activities are estimated as $6.6 million PV over 10 periods.

While there are no accommodations or flexibilities afforded to small businesses in the regulatory proposal, the program was designed with a focus on supporting small businesses in rural areas. The employer requirements identified above have proven successful in supporting the integration and retention of skilled workers in the pilot and supporting pilot participating employers. While these requirements would be in place for all participating employers, they are key contributing factors in meeting the program’s objectives of ensuring successful retention in the region and addressing the labour market needs of these businesses.

One-for-one rule
The one-for-one rule applies, as the regulatory amendments will impose an incremental increase in the administrative burden on Canadian businesses, and the amendments will be considered a burden "in" under the rule. Specifically, the permanent RCI Program will require that employers be formally designated in order to participate in the program. This will result in an additional annualized cost of $15,124 (in 2012 Canadian dollars and discounted to 2012 at 7% annually) for administrative burden, as estimated using the Red Tape Reduction Regulations ’ prescribed method. No regulatory titles are repealed or introduced.
For this proposal, costs to businesses will be limited to those incurred by employers who wish to participate in the permanent RCI Program. Therefore, for the purpose of the one-for-one rule, only impacts incurred by the estimated additional volume of prospective RCI Program employers are considered.
The implementation of a permanent RCI Program will increase the administrative burden on new participating employers, as they will be required to complete application forms to submit their requests for designation. It is therefore estimated that the regulatory amendments will impact approximately 260 employers each year, with each business requiring approximately 6 hours to complete the administrative requirements for designation. The work is assumed to be completed by a human resources administrator, with an average wage rate of $26.98. The annualized administrative cost per business is therefore estimated as $58.
Regulatory cooperation and alignment
An assessment of regulations in other relevant jurisdictions was undertaken, and it was determined that the Regulations do not interfere with any other regulatory initiatives. This proposal aligns with requirements set out in other economic immigration classes, such as the Atlantic Immigration Program and the Provincial Nominee Program.
International obligations
This proposal is not linked to any international agreements or obligations.
Effects on the environment
In accordance with the Cabinet Directive on Strategic Environmental and Economic Assessment (SEEA Directive), a preliminary scan concluded that a SEEA is not required. This economic immigration program is intended to address labour needs in communities that are historically underserved by immigration. The impacts of this program are limited to modest demographic increases in small communities and the potential for economic development in rural and remote economies. Given the limited scope and impact of this program, any new or existing environmental impacts are extremely unlikely.
Gender-based analysis plus
Participating rural and remote communities often face persistent socio-economic challenges, including aging populations, youth outmigration, labour shortages across a range of skill levels, and more limited access to services compared to urban centres. The resulting socio-economic challenges and limited economic opportunities can disproportionately affect women, Indigenous peoples, youth, and other equity-seeking groups, who may face greater barriers to entry into the workforce ( Report of the Standing Committee on the Status of Women (PDF) , June 2021). The RCI Program is designed to attract a diverse population of candidates at all skill levels (TEER 0 5) from various socio-economic and cultural backgrounds. The initiative supports the full economic participation of all, including women and underrepresented groups, by targeting a wide range of occupations through the prioritization exercise and welcoming applicants who can fill these roles from diverse backgrounds, while strengthening cultural diversity and economic resilience in participating communities.
Newcomers tend to be younger and more ethnically diverse than the existing population in many rural and remote communities. This supports population growth and labour force renewal but also highlights the importance of inclusive community services and social integration supports. Principal applicants may bring spouses and family members, which further necessitates consideration of family-oriented services, including childcare, schooling, health care, and language training, particularly for women who may face additional labour market barriers. The Regulations will require participating employers to support candidates with accessing settlement services, including access to language training, childcare, transportation support, and assistance navigating essential systems such as education, financial services, and health care. These measures aim to promote equity, social inclusion, and long-term retention. Participating communities in similar programs have historically often emphasized the importance of integration supports to help newcomers and their families participate fully in community life.
Based on RNIP and RCI Pilot data (noting that RCI Pilot is only 2025), from 2020 to 2025, approximately 54% of admissions identified as male and 46% identified as female, based on available binary sex indicators collected at the time of application. The top source countries for participation in the pilots are India, the Philippines, and Nigeria with China, Ukraine, and Brazil amongst the top 10 source countries. The pilots are open to those of all ages and under the RNIP and RCI Pilot most newcomers were aged between 22 and 31 years old (2020 2025). Compared to the existing population in many participating rural and remote communities where the median age is often higher and populations are often less ethnically diverse RCI Pilot and RNIP newcomers tend to be younger and more racially, ethnically, and culturally diverse. This demographic difference supports population growth and labour force renewal, while also highlighting the need for inclusive community services and social integration supports.
Participating communities have expressed the importance of integration efforts by established newcomers and ethnocultural communities, as well as settlement supports, to help connect newcomers and their families to community life. Some examples of these practices include access to language training, childcare, and transportation supports to address limited public transit options in rural communities, as well as assistance navigating essential systems such as education, financial services and health care. Additional settlement supports may include airport pickups and connections to community and cultural activities that promote social inclusion and long-term retention. IRCC will continue to work with participating communities and share best practices to ensure that equity and diversity practices are maintained when evaluating designated employer and recommendation applications. When possible, through existing channels, IRCC will collect information in order to monitor equity outcomes and ensure continuous improvement for the program.
Rationale
The Government of Canada has committed to attract the best talent in the world to help build Canada’s economy, while returning Canada’s overall immigration rates to sustainable levels. This program will support this priority by ensuring that an immigration pathway exists for those looking to contribute to key national economic priorities, such as construction and health care, while also supporting communities traditionally underserved by immigration, thus alleviating the pressure on larger urban centres to welcome and support newcomers.
Implementation, compliance and enforcement, and service standards
The Regulations will come into effect upon registration.
While the Minister of Citizenship and Immigration will be accountable for the administration of the permanent program, it will be implemented by IRCC in close partnership with participating partner communities. Memorandums of Understanding (MOUs) between IRCC and each of the participating communities will be developed. The MOUs are standard across all communities, outlining the roles and responsibilities of both parties. Similar to the pilot phase, communities will continue to be responsible for the designation of employers and issuance of recommendations to candidates that meet the requirements of the program. Annual priority setting and program integrity will be shared responsibilities between communities and IRCC. IRCC will continue to be responsible for the overall management of the program, the assessment of temporary and permanent residence applications, final selection, admissibility decisions, and temporary and permanent visa issuance, as well as performance management, monitoring, and evaluation. Processing standards will be in line with other permanent residence programs under the economic class.
Communities are selected through a standardized Expression of Interest process. The Expression of Interest application process is open to all communities that meet the eligibility requirements of the program as set out in the Regulations.
Prior to the launch of the permanent program, IRCC will coordinate with the participating communities to ensure a smooth transition from the pilot to the permanent program. Communities will have until December 31, 2026, to finalize all existing recommendations under the RCI Pilot. Given the six-month validity of a recommendation under the pilot, the RCI Pilot Ministerial Instructions (MIs) will remain in force until June 30, 2027, so that candidates who were recommended under the MIs are still able to apply to IRCC under them. As of January 1, 2027, allocations will be issued to communities under the new program, and communities may begin issuing program recommendations to candidates. The intent of this approach is to be facilitative and allow candidates to apply under the program eligibility that they were assessed for in the recommendation phase. Communications activities will also take place to inform the public of the program launch and a full suite of communication products will be prepared to accompany the announcement. Web information and toolkits will also be prepared to provide employers and candidates with support in understanding the transition from pilot to program and navigating the new aspects of the program. After the program launch, IRCC will monitor the performance of the program as part of its commitment to program integrity and client service. It will also continue to meet regularly with the participating communities to ensure effective program governance.
